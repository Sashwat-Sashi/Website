The Citizenship Amendment Act: Beyond the Headlines

The Citizenship Amendment Act (CAA), passed by the Indian Parliament in December 2019, has been the subject of polarised discourse within India and abroad. Critics often raise concerns of exclusion, discrimination, and communalism. However, while legitimate concerns do exist, this article seeks to shed light on underexplored aspects of the Act—namely its use as a tool of soft power, its implicit influence on population dynamics, and its implications for India’s refugee policy.

Soft Power and Foreign Policy

India’s neighbourhood, particularly Afghanistan, Pakistan, and Bangladesh, has witnessed systematic persecution of minority communities such as Hindus, Sikhs, Christians, Parsis, and Buddhists. The Ahmadiyyas in Pakistan [4], Hazaras in Afghanistan [5], and Uyghurs in China [13] all testify to broader regional instability and human rights challenges. By offering a pathway to citizenship to persecuted minorities from neighbouring Islamic states, the CAA repositions India as a moral anchor in South Asia—akin to how the West positions itself with respect to refugees from conflict zones.

Foreign policy has long operated at the intersection of values and strategic interest. The Act strengthens India’s moral authority at international forums like the UN and the Raisina Dialogue [12], bolstering its critique of regional adversaries. Statements by Indian officials referencing Pakistan’s human rights abuses against its own minorities [11] must be seen in this context. While critics may deride the Act for its selectivity, such targeting actually sharpens India’s diplomatic messaging.

Population Redistribution and Domestic Politics

Another underappreciated dimension of the CAA lies in the potential redistribution of population. States like Punjab and Delhi, already home to sizeable Hindu and Sikh refugees from Pakistan and Afghanistan [19], could benefit from regularisation of these communities. Meanwhile, strategically sensitive regions in the Northeast have been explicitly excluded from the Act’s purview to protect indigenous demographics [1].

Opposition to the Act, while rooted in democratic dissent, has often failed to engage with these nuances. The depiction of all refugees as "illegal immigrants" creates a homogenising discourse, erasing distinctions between economic migrants and victims of ethno-religious violence [7]. Furthermore, protests that erupted nationwide in 2019-20, while reflective of civil liberty concerns, often ignored the ground realities faced by persecuted communities [6].

Redefining Refugee Policy

India has no formal refugee law, and asylum seekers are often dealt with on an ad-hoc basis. The CAA represents the first serious attempt at codifying protection for specific vulnerable groups. Critics argue that this selectivity violates the secular ethos of the Constitution [2], [3]. However, laws are rarely absolute. For instance, the United States’ Lautenberg Amendment facilitated expedited refugee status for certain religious groups from the Soviet Union and Southeast Asia. The CAA may be viewed in similar light—as a narrowly tailored measure, not a blanket refugee framework.

Indeed, members of the Jain, Christian, and even Parsi communities have supported the Act [18], [22]. For them, the CAA provides overdue recognition and security. Dismissing this legislation outright risks silencing those whose stories have long been marginalised in public discourse.

Conclusion

The Citizenship Amendment Act is not without flaws. But its framing as an authoritarian overreach misses the complex motives behind its enactment. As India continues to evolve as a regional power, balancing moral responsibility with strategic interests will remain a delicate task. In assessing the CAA, one must move beyond headlines and engage with its multifaceted implications.
