The Citizenship Amendment Act: Beyond the Headlines

By Sashi — Independent Journalist, Canada

Website

Published: Saturday, 19 April 2025

The Citizenship Amendment Act (hereafter CAA) has proven controversial and attracted widespread media attention; albeit from a limited and narrow perspective. Important factors such as soft power dynamics in South Asia, population control, and illegal immigration are often overlooked, despite their significant implications when viewed holistically. Notably, many reputed international media outlets have conveniently excluded these elements—presumably due to conflicts with prevailing public opinion or national interests. This article seeks to highlight these overlooked aspects and present an alternative perspective on the CAA.

Part II of the Indian Constitution[9] outlines eligibility for Indian citizenship based on ancestry, place of birth, and duration of residence. The CAA amends the Indian Citizenship Act of 1955[16] to facilitate the naturalization of religious minorities facing persecution in Afghanistan, Pakistan, and Bangladesh (formerly East Pakistan). However, the legislation notably excludes persecuted Muslim minorities such as the Hazaras[14] and Ahmadis[8,6], who have historically faced systemic discrimination in Pakistan. This selective inclusion has been widely interpreted as religious discrimination, leading to nationwide protests, acts of vandalism, and civil unrest in several Indian states[7].

Yet, beneath this apparent religious bias lies a broader political recalibration within the Indian subcontinent. Historically, India has refrained from publicly addressing the persecution of Shia Muslims, Christians, and Sikhs in neighbouring Islamic states[22]. In contrast, countries like Pakistan and the Western powers have consistently taken the opportunity to comment on or exacerbate internal Indian issues. Over the past decade, however, Indian foreign policy has evolved to confront external criticism and highlight perceived double standards[11,12,10]. This includes raising concerns about the disappearance of Baloch activists in Pakistan[5,3], or the ethnic cleansing of Uyghur Muslims in China[1,15]; issues that were previously left unaddressed.

This shift reflects a broader change in global power dynamics. Throughout history, the promotion and support of persecuted factions within relatively authoritarian regimes has served as a tool of influence—particularly among global superpowers and intelligence agencies in the West. Support, whether material or symbolic, often functions as a strategic form of soft power. In democratic societies, such actions resonate with public sentiment and reinforce a nation’s image as a defender of human rights, indirectly influencing electoral behaviour. Simultaneously, backing dissident groups in hostile nations sends a deterrent message that interference comes at a cost. The CAA, in this context, appears to serve a dual function—both as a humanitarian gesture and as a strategic lever aimed at India’s adversarial neighbours.

What is notably different and often overlooked, is that the public sentiment the CAA seeks to appease extends beyond the Hindu majority to include the bigger minority[2] groups within India[21,18]. For example, Sikhism is the majority religion in Punjab[20], a state with deep historical and emotional ties to the Partition of 1947. Many Sikh families were divided by that event, and for some, the CAA offers not just a legal mechanism for reunification but also a long-overdue acknowledgement of the persecution faced by Sikhs in neighbouring countries[23,17] and within India. Similarly in the northeast, Christianity comprises a substantial portion, if not the majority, of the local population[19]. For many residents, the bill can be perceived less as a discriminatory law and more as a protective measure: a legal framework for identifying and disenfranchising illegal immigrants, thereby securing local demography and cultural identity[4]. The Parsi community, historically known for having fled religious persecution in Persia centuries ago[13], might interpret the CAA as a legal continuation of India’s tradition of providing refuge to the oppressed. From this perspective, the Act can be seen as affirming the country’s long-standing civilizational ethos of pluralism and shelter.

However, the policy’s full potential remains underutilized. To truly serve as a national interest tool, the CAA should be refined and paired with clear messaging and effective coordination with state governments. Rather than being seen as selectively inclusive, it could serve as a deterrent to illegal immigration. By making it explicit that unauthorized entry will lead to disenfranchisement, persecuted minorities may find refuge, the policy would strike a clearer balance between compassion and security.

Finally, setting aside the soft power dynamics and geopolitical signaling, one must ask: does India genuinely need to absorb additional refugees through legislation like the CAA? Especially when the country is already facing immense demographic pressures, with densely populated urban centers, uneven resource distribution, and a lack of coordinated population control policies? India is far from being the most capable or resource-abundant nation in the world. There are countries with stronger economies, lower population densities, and greater infrastructure capacity that arguably bear a greater responsibility to offer refuge to the persecuted. Yet, India has taken on this burden—largely alone. This raises an uncomfortable but necessary question: India continues to offer shelter and citizenship on moral and historical grounds, does it come at the cost of domestic well-being, resource strain, and future stability? The conversation around the CAA, therefore, must evolve beyond legal specifics and religious implications to consider a bigger picture that includes population control, soft power pertinent to national security, and the state of illegal migration.
